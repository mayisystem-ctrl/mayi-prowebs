Landing Page vs. Website - What's the Difference, and When Each One Is Right for Your Business

Short answer: a landing page drives one person from one source to one action; a website builds a full presence that serves different audiences and entry points over time. A landing page fits a focused campaign with a clear offer; a website fits when you want to build organic search presence and serve everyone who arrives - not just the people who came from one ad. The right choice depends on the stage your business is at right now - not a trend, and not what your competitor built.

Why you even need to decide

Both tools use the same technical language - HTML, design, a lead form - so it's easy to confuse them. But their job is fundamentally different. A landing page is built to drive one person, who arrived from a specific place (a campaign, an ad, a link), to one action. A website is built to serve a varied audience arriving from different sources - a Google search, a referral, a return visit - and give them everything they need to choose you.

Landing page - when it's the right tool

  • You have one clear offer you want to test or sell fast (a paid campaign, a launch, a one-off event).
  • Traffic comes from one known source (a Facebook ad, a Google campaign) rather than broad organic search.
  • The goal is fast conversion - not building trust over time.
  • You want to measure and improve conversion rate without navigation that distracts.

Per Unbounce's Conversion Benchmark Report (Q4 2024, based on 464 million visits across 41,000 landing pages), the median landing page conversion rate is around 6.6% - typically 2.35%-10.76% depending on industry. That's why a focused landing page almost always beats a general homepage when the goal is converting paid campaign traffic (source: unbounce.com).

Website - when it's the right tool

  • The business needs a presence that represents it fully - not one offer, but everything it provides.
  • You want to build organic presence in Google and AI search engines (ChatGPT, Perplexity) over time.
  • There are several different audiences that need different paths to information (customers, partners, recruiters).
  • Trust builds gradually - the customer wants to explore, compare, and come back.

You can have both

Many businesses we work with at May I actually start with a single landing page - to test a message, a market, an offer - and only after it proves itself do they move to a full website. That's the right path: you invest in a broader site after you know the message works, not before.

What definitely isn't a good idea

The most common mistake: building a website and trying to run a paid campaign straight to the homepage. Paid traffic needs to land on a page focused on one goal - not a homepage with ten links splitting attention. And the reverse: a business that wants to build organic search presence over time can't get by on a single landing page - Google and AI engines don't have enough content to crawl and cite. A study by Kevin Indig on ChatGPT citations found that 44.2% of citations come from the first 30% of a document - meaning even when content exists, if the direct answer is buried deep inside, AI engines simply don't reach it (source: searchengineland.com).
