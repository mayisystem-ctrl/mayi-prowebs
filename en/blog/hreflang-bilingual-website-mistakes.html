Broken Hreflang on a Bilingual Site - The Technical Mistake That Makes Google and AI Show the Wrong Version of Your Website

Short answer: hreflang is an HTML tag that declares to Google which language versions of a page exist and where each one lives - and per a study covered by Search Engine Land, 31% of international websites have a hreflang error. On a Hebrew-English site, that means in practice that Google can show an English-speaking searcher the Hebrew version of a page (or the reverse), and that AI engines like ChatGPT simply skip the version they're not confident about. The fix itself is fairly simple and technical - the problem is that most sites don't even know it's broken.

Why this happens at all

To Google, every Hebrew-English site is really two completely separate sites - a Hebrew page and an English page are two different URLs, and without explicit markup there is nothing linking them as versions of the same content. Hreflang is how you tell Google "this page and that page are the same content, in two languages - show the visitor whichever one fits them." When the tag is missing, wrong, or doesn't match in both directions, Google simply guesses - and sometimes that guess is wrong.

The most common mistakes (and what they actually do)

  • Missing self-reference: a Hebrew page must also declare itself as the hreflang="he" version, not just point to its English counterpart. Without this, Google ignores the entire hreflang block on that page.
  • One-way linking: the Hebrew page points to the English page, but the English page doesn't point back. Google requires full two-way agreement - otherwise it treats the tag as if it weren't there.
  • Missing x-default: no fallback is declared for a visitor whose language is neither Hebrew nor English. Without it, Google picks for itself, and not always correctly.
  • Hreflang and canonical disagreeing: the Hebrew page's canonical mistakenly points to the English page. That's a contradictory signal that makes Google largely ignore the Hebrew page.
  • Invalid language codes: he-IL written as heb, or en written as eng. A tag that isn't valid ISO 639-1 simply isn't read.

Why this affects AI engines too, not just Google

Answer engines like ChatGPT and Perplexity don't build their own index from scratch - they lean heavily on crawl signals and authority that already exist in Google and in the sources they do crawl directly. A page Google is confused about (because its hreflang is broken) earns less authority, and less chance of being fully crawled when the direct answer sits deep in the content. A study covered by Search Engine Land found that 44.2% of ChatGPT's citations come from the first 30% of a document - so even a site with correct hreflang still needs the direct answer sitting up top, not just the right page in the right language getting crawled at all.

How to check whether you have a problem

You don't need expensive tools. A one-time crawl with Screaming Frog (the free version is enough for a mid-sized site) shows a dedicated hreflang tab flagging exactly which pages are missing a self-reference, missing x-default, or have one-way linking. The most reliable real-world signal is simpler: a client or visitor reports landing on the wrong version from a Google search - that's almost always broken hreflang, not a content problem.

How to build it correctly from the start

  • Every page carries three alternate tags: he, en, and x-default - including a reference to itself and to its counterpart.
  • Every page's canonical points to itself, never to the other-language version.
  • A consistent URL structure between versions (e.g. /blog/slug vs. /en/blog/slug) so the pairing is easy to maintain and hard to get wrong.
  • When a new page goes up in one language, the reverse tag goes into its counterpart in the same breath - not a separate cleanup pass later.

Why this happens far more often on builder sites

Wix, Squarespace, and most builders only handle hreflang through a built-in multilingual module, usually with no way to see or edit the tag directly - if the module implements it wrong, there's no simple manual fix. In custom code, the tag is written explicitly on every page, verified by crawl, and fully controlled - including edge cases like x-default that most builders simply skip.
