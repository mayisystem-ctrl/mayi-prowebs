Bilingual Business Website (Hebrew-English) - hreflang, International SEO, and Why It's Not Just Translation

Short answer: a properly built bilingual site is two separate content versions, on separate URLs, linked to each other with reciprocal hreflang tags in the head - not a translation widget layered over one site. Google requires full reciprocal linking between versions to understand they're translations of the same content rather than competing duplicate pages, per official Google Search Central documentation. And for anyone wondering if it's worth the effort - a global survey of 8,709 consumers across 29 countries found that 76% prefer buying a product with information in their native language, and 40% simply won't buy from a site in another language (source: CSA Research).

Why it's not just "translating the site"

Many businesses we work with come to us after already trying a quick fix - a translation widget that adds an "EN" button in the corner. It looks like a solution, but technically it's almost always a problem: the translated text runs in client-side JavaScript, usually isn't stored on a separate URL that can be linked to, so Google doesn't crawl it as an independent page and can't show it in search results to English speakers. Beyond that - mechanical translation doesn't control tone, doesn't choose the keywords people actually search for in English, and sometimes just sounds off. A real bilingual site is two separate writing experiences, each speaking to its own audience.

What hreflang actually does, and the technical rules you can't get wrong

hreflang is a <link> tag added to the head of every page that tells Google: "this content also exists in another language, and here's its URL." Three rules you can't skip:

  • Full reciprocal linking - the Hebrew page must point to the English page, and the English page must point back to the Hebrew one. A one-way link isn't valid and Google may ignore it.
  • Self-reference - every page also includes an hreflang tag pointing to itself, not just to the other version.
  • x-default - an additional tag telling Google which version to show a visitor whose language doesn't match either option (say, a French speaker landing on the site).

Here's what that looks like in real code, on this very site:

  • <link rel="alternate" hreflang="he" href="https://mayi.dev/prowebs/blog/bilingual-website-hreflang-seo">
  • <link rel="alternate" hreflang="en" href="https://mayi.dev/prowebs/en/blog/bilingual-website-hreflang-seo">
  • <link rel="alternate" hreflang="x-default" href="https://mayi.dev/prowebs/blog/bilingual-website-hreflang-seo">

The common mistakes we've actually seen

It's almost never one big mistake, but several small ones that add up: an hreflang link pointing to an old URL that's been removed, a one-way link (Hebrew points to English but not the reverse), a forgotten x-default, or hreflang tags injected via JavaScript that aren't always read at crawl time. The problem with all of these is that they're silent - the site still looks fine to a visitor, but Google interprets the two versions as competing content instead of complementary content, and ranks both worse for it.

URL structure - a language folder, not a parameter

The most stable approach is a separate path folder per language (for example /blog/ for Hebrew and /en/blog/ for English), with the same slug structure across both versions. It's consistent, easy to track in Google Search Console as two separate "sites" for search data, and simpler to maintain as you add new content - every Hebrew page gets an English counterpart at the matching path.

How this affects AI engines too, not just Google

hreflang is a standard Google and traditional search engines officially support - there's no official confirmation that engines like ChatGPT or Perplexity read it the same way. But in practice, when someone asks a question in English, the engine tends to cite content originally written in English and that reads naturally in it - not a literal translation. And it connects to a broader rule we've written about before: a study tracking ChatGPT citations found that 44.2% of citations come from the first 30% of a document (source: searchengineland.com) - meaning in any language, the direct answer has to be in the opening, not buried in the tenth paragraph. On a bilingual site, that adds another requirement: you can't "translate the opening later" as an afterthought - each language version needs a direct, well-written opening in its own right.

When it's worth the extra investment

Not every business needs a bilingual site. If all your customers speak Hebrew and you have no plans to reach an English-speaking market (tourists, international customers, an immigrant community), the budget is better spent deepening the Hebrew content. But if you have a real audience in both languages - say, a Tel Aviv business serving both Israeli residents and an English-speaking immigrant or tourist community - a properly built English version, not a widget, is the difference between being found in search and being invisible.
