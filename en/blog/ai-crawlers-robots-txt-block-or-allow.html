Should You Block AI Crawlers in robots.txt? The Business Decision Behind a Technical Checkbox

Short answer: there's no single rule that fits every business, but a blanket block of "all AI bots" in robots.txt is almost always a mistake - it erases you from ChatGPT and Perplexity answers without providing real protection, since robots.txt is a voluntary request, not a technical lock. The right decision isn't "block or don't block" - it's telling apart a bot that collects content to train a model from a bot that fetches a live answer for someone asking about you right now, and blocking, if anything, only the first kind.

What robots.txt actually does - and why it isn't a lock

robots.txt is a plain text file sitting at the root of a domain that asks specific robots not to crawl specific paths. It's a voluntary request, not a firewall. A crawler that states it respects the protocol - like OpenAI's GPTBot - chooses to comply. A tool that doesn't identify itself at all, or simply has no intention of honoring the request, can ignore it completely. It's also worth remembering that robots.txt only applies to the domain or subdomain it sits on - one file at the site's root doesn't cover a separate subdomain (like blog. or shop.), which needs its own file.

Not every "AI bot" is the same bot

OpenAI, for example, runs at least three separate agents with completely different jobs (source: platform.openai.com):

  • GPTBot - a crawler that collects content that may be used to train future models.
  • OAI-SearchBot - builds the index that powers search answers inside ChatGPT.
  • ChatGPT-User - fetches a page in real time when a user asks ChatGPT about a specific link or topic.

Blocking GPTBot alone doesn't block the other two. A business that blocks "everything OpenAI" as a blanket rule, without telling the three apart, may end up blocking itself from appearing in ChatGPT's search answers - without getting any extra protection in return, since Anthropic, Perplexity, and Google each run their own similar, separate logic.

What the data actually shows

An Ahrefs study that scanned roughly 461 million robots.txt files found that only 7.3% of them actually block GPTBot - meaning the vast majority of sites on the web don't block it, usually because they never made a deliberate decision about it in the first place. The same study found that Anthropic's ClaudeBot is the fastest-growing bot in terms of block rate - up roughly 32.67% over the past year, a sign that more businesses are starting to treat the question deliberately instead of leaving it on default (source: ahrefs.com).

The real business trade-off

If you block OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, or Google-Extended, you're removing yourself from the pool these AI engines draw answers from - including the answer to "who's the best business for X near me." A study by Kevin Indig on ChatGPT citations found that 44.2% of citations come from the first 30% of a document - meaning even a site that blocks nothing but structures its content poorly still loses visibility. A full block simply takes the site out of the game entirely, with no way to win even with the best content on the web (source: searchengineland.com).

On the other hand, some businesses genuinely want to prevent their unique content from being used to train a model - for instance, expensive-to-produce content that is their actual competitive edge. For them, blocking only the training agents (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Google-Extended) without touching the live-answer agents (OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, ChatGPT-User) is a setup that achieves both goals at once.

The practical recommendation

  • Check what your robots.txt actually says today - some templates and security plugins ship with a blanket "AI bots" block by default, without the owner ever making a conscious choice about it.
  • Decide on a goal before you set a rule: want visibility in AI answer engines? Don't block the agents that feed live answers. Worried about a model training on unique content? Block only the named training agents, not a single blanket rule.
  • Remember that robots.txt is a request, not protection - if the goal is to actually prevent content use, not just reduce the odds of it, it isn't the complete tool for that job.
