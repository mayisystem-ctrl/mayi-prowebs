INP - The Click-Responsiveness Metric That Replaced FID in Core Web Vitals, and Why Your Lead Form's Submit Button Is Usually the First to Suffer

Short answer: INP (Interaction to Next Paint) replaced FID as Google's official Core Web Vital on March 12, 2024, and it measures the full time from any click, tap, or keypress on a page - not just the first one, like FID did - until the screen actually responds. A good score is 200 milliseconds or less at the 75th percentile, and anything above 500 milliseconds is considered poor (source: web.dev). The impact on a small business is direct: a site that loads fast but responds sluggishly when someone taps "submit" on a lead form has a real problem that a standard load-speed test simply won't catch.

What INP actually is, and why FID wasn't enough

FID (First Input Delay) measured a narrow thing: how long it took from the moment a user first clicked on a page until the browser even started processing that click. It didn't measure how long the processing itself took, and it ignored every interaction after the first one - including the click that matters most to you, submitting your lead form. INP closes both gaps: it measures the full duration of every interaction on a page - a mouse click, a keypress, a tap - from start until the browser actually paints the next frame, and it does this across a visitor's entire time on the page, not just their first click. Google officially announced that the metric took over from FID on March 12, 2024 (source: web.dev).

What the data actually shows

Per Google's own Chrome UX Report (CrUX), published monthly, about 87% of measured origins have a "good" INP score - but only around 56% of them pass all three Core Web Vitals together (source: developer.chrome.com). In other words, on most sites load speed (LCP) or visual stability (CLS) is usually the bigger blocker, not interaction responsiveness - but that's an origin-wide average, not a measurement of the one specific interaction that actually matters to your business. Per HTTP Archive's Web Almanac, the share of mobile pages passing all three Core Web Vitals rose from 36% in 2023 to 48% in 2025 (source: almanac.httparchive.org) - real progress, but still leaving more than half of sites below the bar.

Where it actually breaks on business websites

  • Drag-and-drop funnel and page builders - every element placed through a visual builder ships with its own JavaScript wrapper and listener. The heavier the page is in elements, the more code runs the instant a visitor clicks.
  • Third-party widgets loaded without delay - chat, a booking calendar embed, a popup - when they load and listen on the main thread synchronously, they can block exactly the moment a visitor clicks "submit."
  • Tracking code wired directly into the click handler - GA4, Meta Pixel, and GoHighLevel tracking events that run extra code before the browser can even paint the "received" state - the one click that matters most to you is the one absorbing the slowdown.
  • Plugin stacking - WordPress with many plugins, each adding its own listener, even when each one individually looks light.

What actually helps

  • Delay loading non-critical widgets (chat, a calendar embed) until after the visitor's first interaction, instead of blocking the main thread with them from the start.
  • Keep your primary CTA's click handler lean - fire secondary tracking code without blocking the visual change the user is waiting to see.
  • Avoid a heavy re-render cascade triggered by a framework on every form submission.
  • Test with real tools - PageSpeed Insights (field data from CrUX) or Chrome's Performance panel - and specifically click your actual CTA, not just measure page load.

This is one of the spots where custom code tends to beat a visual builder or template: fewer stacked third-party scripts, and full control over exactly what runs on which click - instead of the platform's default.
