Should You Block AI Bots (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot) in robots.txt? Training Crawlers vs. Citation Crawlers

Short answer: there's no single "all AI bots" switch to decide on at once. GPTBot and ClaudeBot are training crawlers - blocking them keeps future content out of model training, but doesn't remove you from ChatGPT or Claude's search results. OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, Claude-User, and PerplexityBot are an entirely different category - search and citation bots that fetch content in real time to power the answers users actually receive. A business that wants to be cited by AI can, and usually should, block the training bots without hurting its own visibility - but a blanket block of "anything that sounds like a bot" also removes it from the citations it actually wants.

Why this is confusing - "AI bot" isn't one thing

Every AI company runs several distinct bots, not one. OpenAI has GPTBot (model training), OAI-SearchBot (powers ChatGPT search results), and ChatGPT-User (fetches a page live when a user asks a question). Anthropic has ClaudeBot (training), Claude-SearchBot (indexes content to improve search results), and Claude-User (fetches a page for a specific user request). Perplexity runs PerplexityBot. Each of these is declared separately in robots.txt under its own user-agent name, and blocking one has no effect on the others.

Blocking a training bot doesn't remove you from search

OpenAI's own documentation is explicit: blocking GPTBot is a choice to opt future content out of model training - nothing more. Visibility in ChatGPT's search results is controlled separately by OAI-SearchBot, and a site that blocked only GPTBot keeps appearing in search answers unless it blocked that one too (source: developers.openai.com). Anthropic says the same about its own three bots, each honoring robots.txt on its own: blocking ClaudeBot alone removes you from Claude's future training set without touching Claude-SearchBot or Claude-User, which keep bringing your content into real-time answers (source: support.claude.com).

What most sites on the web actually do

Among major news publishers, blocking training bots is already the norm: a Press Gazette survey of about 100 leading US and UK news sites found roughly 79% block at least one AI training crawler (source: pressgazette.co.uk). But across the broader web the picture looks very different: a HasData sample of roughly 10,894 domains found only about 7.9% block GPTBot specifically and about 10.3% block any AI crawler at all - a far smaller share than among publishers (source: hasdata.com). The trend is still accelerating: per The Register, roughly 5.6 million websites now disallow GPTBot in robots.txt, up from about 3.3 million at the start of July 2025 (source: theregister.com).

The risk of a blanket block - removing yourself from the answer too

Many site owners, especially small businesses, update robots.txt by copy-pasting a "block all AI bots" list without distinguishing the categories - and in doing so block the very search bots they want reaching them. That's a costly mistake twice over: you're not protecting any more content than a targeted block would (training bots and search bots are separate entities), and you've also disappeared from the answers themselves. That matters because a study tracking ChatGPT citations found that 44.2% of citations come from the first 30% of a document (source: searchengineland.com) - meaning even when a citation bot is unblocked and reaches your site, content structure decides whether it actually pulls an answer from it. Blocking that same bot by mistake erases the chance entirely, before the structure question is even relevant.

Why there's a real business trade-off here

Cloudflare's own data explains why large publishers specifically distinguish between the categories instead of blocking everything. As of July 2025, Anthropic's crawl-to-refer ratio stood at roughly 38,000 crawled pages for every one referred visit, compared to about 1,091 for OpenAI over the same period (source: blog.cloudflare.com). Training bots crawl heavily and send back very little real traffic - that's the practical case for blocking them. Search and citation bots are the traffic and visibility channel itself, which makes blocking them an entirely different decision, not "one more AI category."

The practical recommendation - what to actually do in robots.txt

  • Map the exact bots relevant to your business by precise user-agent name - not "block AI" as one category.
  • Decide on training bots (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, and similar) separately from search/citation bots (OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, Claude-User, PerplexityBot).
  • Write a separate user-agent rule for each bot you want blocked - there's no single blanket rule that correctly catches all of them.
  • Test robots.txt after every change, and re-check it every few months - AI companies add and change bots at a fast pace.
